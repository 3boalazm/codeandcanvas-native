From Brand Identity to Brand Systems

A static manual is not a brand. The moment a second team touches your identity, the question stops being "does the logo look right" and becomes "does the system make the right decision without us?"

Published in Arabic and English · standalone Arabic page.

Brand Identity · Code & Canvas ILLUSTRATIVE ARTWORK · NOT EVIDENCE

The problem with a static manual

A brand manual arrives as a finished file: a logo, colours, type and a handful of perfect applications. For a few weeks everything looks controlled. Then the company launches a new product, recruiting needs a deck template, a different supplier produces the packaging, and a regional office opens a social page — and the manual answers none of those questions.

The result is not only visual drift. It is decisions made by accident: who owns a change, whether the lockup may be adjusted for a narrow space, whether the brand is translated or rebuilt in Arabic. If the answer is not written down, the answer is whoever speaks loudest in the meeting.

An identity is a set of assets. A brand system is a set of decisions.

What makes an identity a system

A system is not defined by page count. It is defined by three properties: repeatable rules, executable tokens, and governance that says who decides. With those three, a team that never attended the founding workshop can produce a new asset that looks like it came from the same source.

  1. Rules derived from a decision

    Colour is not chosen because it is attractive; it is chosen because the positioning needs a particular signal in the market. Every visual rule traces back to a stated commercial or functional reason.

  2. Executable tokens

    Spacing, elevation, states and colour are defined once and consumed in Figma, in code and in print files. Changing one token propagates everywhere instead of being corrected by hand in forty files.

  3. Governance and decision rights

    Who approves a new application? What may never change? When is the positioning reopened? These are operational questions, not questions of taste.

Bilingual is structural, not cosmetic

In our markets, Arabic is not a layer added after the English design is finished. Reading direction, word length, letter height, numeral direction and punctuation all affect the grid itself. If the English lockup is built first, the Arabic version ends up stretched or squeezed — and both are wrong.

The correct process is one geometric grid, a stated proportional relationship between the two lockups, and minimum-size rules for each application. The brand is then not translated; it is built twice from the same decision.

The handover is the product

The most neglected part of identity work is the day after handover. Files alone do not transfer reasoning. That is why the handover session, the short governance guide and an explicit "what we do not do" list are part of the deliverable, not an extra.

✓
A simple test

Give a team that did not attend the project a brief for an application the manual never covered. If they come back asking "which blue do we use?", the system is incomplete. If they return with the right application and one governance question, the system works.

Where to start

Start with an inventory of every touchpoint the organization produces today, and classify each: keep, change, remove. Then write the positioning on one page before opening a design file. Only then build the system. A different order usually means rebuilding the work six months later.
